# Sprint Work
*<font color="#8b949e">Commit to work you can actually deliver — then prove it works</font>*

<font color="#a371f7">Reinforce</font>

</div>

---

Over the next three classes your team runs three **sprints**: short, fixed-length work cycles where each person commits to a piece of work at the start and delivers it by the end. Today — and every sprint day — you are going to commit to work, then do the work.

Whatever you commit to, make sure it is a commitment you can **deliver on** and that it is the **highest-priority** work available. Choosing what to commit to is a skill of its own, called project planning.

---

## <font color="#388bfd">Project planning</font>

Before anyone writes code, the team plans the sprint:

1. **Identify the most important tasks to do**
   - At companies this is often organized in Jira, on a Kanban board, or on a GitHub project board
2. **Estimate the amount of time each task will take**
   - If a task is ***too*** complex to estimate confidently, break it down into smaller tasks
3. **Assign tasks to each person**
   - At a company, a product manager normally does this — on your team, do it together and record the assignments as issues

> **Warning:**
> Make sure you can *have your work done* **2+ hours before the next class**. A commitment that lands at 11:59 the night before leaves no time to review, integrate, or recover when something breaks.

---

## <font color="#388bfd">How sprint work is graded</font>

Each sprint is graded on evidence, not effort:

- **Did you finish your portion and commit the work to GitHub?**
- **Is there proof that it worked?** How can you *prove* it is working?
  - Are there things that are not working correctly?
  - Have more bugs been identified — or introduced?
  - Does it only *kind of* work?

> **Warning:**
> If you generated code with AI, did not test it, and it does not work, the maximum grade you can earn for that sprint is **50%**. Untested AI output is not a deliverable — it is a guess.

---

## <font color="#388bfd">What if you cannot finish?</font>

Sometimes a whole pile of unexpected details comes up and the task turns out harder than planned. You have two very different paths, and they are graded very differently:

| Path | What it looks like | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Silent failure** | You committed nothing in class and did not ask for help while there was still reasonable time remaining | Learn to start work earlier in class and ask questions — this costs you |
| **2. Honest re-planning** | You called out early that the work *may* take longer than planned, then split the task into (a) the part you CAN and will do and (b) the part you push out until later | **You will not be downgraded for this** |

This is the deliverable discipline from [Deliverables and Issue Tracking](../DeliverablesAndIssueTracking/) applied under time pressure: split the issue, deliver the part you can, document the rest.

> **Tip:**
> Path 2 is exactly what professional engineers do. Flagging a slipping task early is a sign of competence, not weakness — it lets the team re-plan while there is still time.

---

## <font color="#388bfd">Reviewing as part of the sprint</font>

A sprint deliverable is not just your own code. Starting with the second sprint, your deliverable also includes at least one **pull request you reviewed** for a teammate. Reviewing is sprint work: verify the behavior, leave notes, and merge the work you approve.

## <font color="#388bfd">Skill Building</font>

### <font color="#79c0ff">Introductory</font>

- [ ] Can you list the three steps of project planning in order?
- [ ] Can you state the deadline rule for sprint commitments (how long before next class must work be done)?
- [ ] Can you say what the maximum grade is for untested AI-generated code that does not work?

### <font color="#79c0ff">Intermediate</font>

- [ ] Can you explain what to do when a task is too complex to estimate confidently?
- [ ] Can you describe the two paths available when you cannot finish committed work, and which one avoids a downgrade?
- [ ] Can you name at least two forms of proof that your sprint work actually runs?

### <font color="#79c0ff">Advanced</font>

- [ ] Can you plan a full sprint for your team — prioritized tasks, time estimates, breakdowns, and per-person assignments recorded as issues?
- [ ] Can you split a slipping task into a deliverable part and a deferred part, and update the issues to match?
- [ ] Can you complete a sprint that includes both your own delivered branch and a meaningful review of a teammate's pull request?
